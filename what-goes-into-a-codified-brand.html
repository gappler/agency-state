The practice

What goes into a codified brand

What the source holds, what stays out, and why.

The inventory

This is the working inventory I start from when codifying a brand, in three tiers: required for anything to run on the source, recommended for a strong build, optional when the material exists.

Identity and architecture

Brand books cover this part best: the name, the canonical lines, how the products relate. A machine still needs each stated rather than implied: what the name means, which line goes where.

identity: required: - brand name and usage rules - canonical lines (tagline, positioning line, preview line), each with provenance recommended: - values as stated in the brand book - mission or purpose, if the brand uses one architecture: recommended: - product and sub-brand naming structure - what gets capitalized; what is never abbreviated

Positioning

The positioning statement names the target audience, the category, and the differentiators, with each differentiator a claim with its basis.

positioning: required: - the positioning statement - audience definition; category - differentiators, as claims with their basis optional: - named competitor facts, for pages that compare

Positions

This is the part I most often find unwritten, and it is what gives copy a point of view.

positions: recommended: - what the brand believes, with an owner and a date - the category debates it takes a side in - what it deliberately disagrees with

Voice

Voice codifies in two forms. Data points capture the measurable habits: word choice, sentence length, reading level, punctuation. Exemplars are different: three to five whole passages the team agrees sound most like the brand, kept verbatim and served before drafting, because exemplars carry what adjectives cannot.

voice: required: - preferred and banned words - sentence-length tendency; reading level - person (first / second / third); punctuation habits - the style guide of record (AP, Chicago, or house), and the brand's exceptions to it - exemplars: 3-5 passages, verbatim, with source labels recommended: - register splits (when the brand writes formally vs. plainly) - per-channel variations, stated as differences from the core voice

Visual identity

Exact values are what make the visual brand checkable: a machine can catch an off-brand color or typeface on its own.

visual: required: - palette, with exact values - typefaces and weights - logo usage rules recommended: - spacing and layout, stated as checkable rules - imagery and illustration direction

Products and claims

Copy leans hardest on the product facts: the specifications and names a draft may state, each with status attached. When a draft cites a product or makes a claim, a source check asks whether what the draft names is actually on file, so the answer depends on this part being complete.

products: required: - the product list, with status (active, retired) - the facts copy may state: specifications, names - approved claims per product, each with its conditions

Approved language

Boilerplate and legal text are stored as exact reusable blocks, so a machine repeats what was approved instead of paraphrasing it. The best published passages belong here too: language that already worked, kept on hand.

approved-language: required: - company descriptions at standard lengths (25 / 50 / 100 / full) - legal and disclosure language; required attributions recommended: - the best published passages, each with provenance and where it may run

Stories

Stories go in as separate pieces: where the company came from, the customer stories worth retelling, the mistakes it learned from. Each is tagged, so the right one gets served for the right job instead of everything sitting in one long document.

stories: recommended: - origin story - named case anecdotes - the mistakes it learned from - tags per entry: topic, audience, length

Rules

Every rule says where it came from, and whether its wording is verbatim, inferred, or decided. A rule without a source goes back for a decision before it enters the system.

rules: required: - the never-say list - claim conditions (what may be said, when) - formatting rules recommended: - conditional rules (allowed only under set conditions), stated precisely enough to check every rule records: - source: where it came from - basis: verbatim / inferred / decided

Decision rights and the review ledger

Decision rights name who decides what: voice, claims, visual exceptions, legal. When the machine hits a question it cannot settle, the question goes to that person. Open items collect in the review ledger, filed as needs-review: inferences drawn from the materials, conflicts found in them, decisions left for the team.

decision-rights: required: - who owns voice decisions, claim approvals, visual exceptions, legal review needs-review: required: - inferences made during codification - conflicts found - decisions left for a person

What stays out

Prices go in as a placeholder that routes to a person, never as a stored value; otherwise the system keeps serving an old number as if it were current. The same goes for anything that changes faster than a source stays trustworthy. Voice scoring is out too: whether a draft sounds like the brand is a judgment, and a person makes it.

The gap report

Scoring a brand against this inventory, slot by slot (filled, partial, or empty), produces a gap report: what codifies today, what needs a decision first, and what this brand does not need. Scored that way, the report maps where a brand's decisions already live and where they are still waiting to be made.
